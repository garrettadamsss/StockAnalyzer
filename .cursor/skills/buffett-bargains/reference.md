# Moat and management reference

> "The key to investing is not assessing how much an industry is going to affect society, or how much it will grow, but rather determining the competitive advantage of any given company, and above all the durability of that advantage. The products or services that have wide, sustainable moats around them are the ones that deliver rewards to investors."

## The nine moat categories

### Brand moat

Company's name and reputation are so strong that customers repeatedly choose it over competitors, even when similar or cheaper alternatives exist. Usually built over many years by well established companies. Built on psychology and can be one of the most powerful.

Examples: Apple, Ferrari, lululemon.

### Switching moat

Company's product or service is so deeply embedded in a customer's life or business that switching would be costly, time-consuming, inconvenient or risky. Common with companies that provide software to other companies.

Examples: Microsoft 365, Apple's ecosystem.

### Network effects moat

The more people use the product, the more valuable it becomes to everyone. Similar to switching moat but instead of costing you time and money, it costs you usefulness or enjoyment. Best examples are social media or messaging apps.

Examples: Messenger, WhatsApp, Visa forcing merchants to accept them.

### "Secret sauce" moat

Powerful advantages you can't necessarily see, which include patents, licences, proprietary tech or trade secrets, unique content rights, and ironclad partnerships or contracts.

Examples: Pfizer patents on life saving drugs, Disney and all of its intellectual property (Marvel, Star Wars, Pixar).

### Toll moat

Company controls critical infrastructure or access points and charges others to use it. Most common with physical infrastructure: railroads, toll roads and bridges, pipelines, ports and shipping terminals, airports.

Examples: Visa and Mastercard operate as toll collectors on global card payments; Google charges a toll on every internet search via advertising; Apple's App Store acts as a digital toll booth through commission.

### Cost advantage moat

Company can produce and sell its products or services at a lower cost without compromising quality. Four key ways to achieve it:

1. Economies of scale: producing large volumes brings down the per-unit cost
2. Efficient supply chains: better logistics, sourcing, or production methods
3. Vertical integration: controlling more of the supply chain
4. Technological efficiency: using software or automation to reduce labor and overhead

Example: Costco keeps cost down by limiting product selection and keeping a barebones design, offering lower prices without sacrificing profitability.

### Scale advantage moat

The company becomes so large and efficient that its size actually becomes a competitive advantage. The business gets stronger the bigger it grows, and the scale allows it to operate on thin margins and still profit. Five key benefits:

1. Lower costs per unit: big companies can buy in bulk, ship in bulk, and produce at scale
2. Supplier leverage: they negotiate better deals because of their buying power
3. Wider distribution and market reach: scale allows companies to reach customers more cheaply
4. Brand recognition and trust: larger companies are often more trusted
5. Better data and insights: more customer data allows them to optimize

Examples: Walmart squeezes suppliers for better prices because of its size; Amazon's ecommerce business has one of the most advanced logistics networks in the world.

### Barrier to entry moat

It is about the sheer magnitude of investment and infrastructure a company has built over time. Any new competitor trying to replicate it would be financially unrealistic and strategically unwise, so it is not even worth trying.

Examples: cruise lines, which are extremely capital intensive to enter; railroads, where tracks took years and billions of dollars, plus land acquisition, regulatory hurdles, and environmental approvals.

### Monopoly moat

The ultimate moat, no other competition. You own the market, as long as regulators don't stop you.

Example: Google is a "near monopoly", with roughly 90% of all global search traffic.

## Quantitative tests

### The Standout Test

Whether a company's gross margin exceeds competitors.

```
Gross Margin (%) = (Revenue - Cost of Goods Sold) / Revenue x 100
```

Compare only within the same industry: Walmart has a gross margin of 24% while Google has 58%. Gross margin tells you how efficiently a company makes money, through either pricing power or cost efficiency.

### The Two Engine Test

Growth and profitability together. Operating margin subtracts direct and indirect costs such as research and development, sales and marketing, and administrative staff.

If a company is consistently growing revenue *and* improving margins, it is pulling away from competitors rather than simply growing with the market. What usually happens instead:

1. Improve margins, and revenue often slows (raising prices, reducing staff, cutting advertising, eliminating unprofitable segments)
2. Improve revenue, and margins shrink (attracting new customers and expanding into new markets requires lower prices or more advertising spend)

Criteria: revenue growth of at least 10% over the past 10 years, and operating margin improving over the past 10 years or at least remaining the same.

### The Capital Efficiency Test

How good a company is at investing in itself. Return on invested capital measures how much profit a business earns on each invested dollar, such as building factories, buying equipment, or expanding operations.

```
ROIC = NOPAT / Invested Capital
NOPAT = Operating Income x (1 - Effective Tax Rate)
Invested Capital = Equity + Total Debt
```

Google's roughly 27% ROIC reflects strong pricing power (Google Ads), low capital needs (a digital platform), and a dominant market position. AT&T's roughly 6% reflects a capital intensive business, heavy debt, infrastructure costs, and low pricing power or intense competition.

| ROIC | Read |
|------|------|
| 15% or more | Excellent, indicates a moat |
| 10% to 15% | Solid |
| Under 10% | Weak, no competitive edge |

Criteria: ROIC above 15%, and improving over the past 10 years.

## Assessing management

Finding a moat only gets you halfway to a wonderful business. A moat protects against competition; the quality of the people leading the company matters equally. Look for leaders who think like owners, act with integrity, and focus on long-term value. The CEO carries the most weight of any leadership role, and you are placing trust in that person as much as in the business.

Two core traits:

- **Integrity**: is the person honest, transparent, trustworthy? Do they communicate openly with shareholders, admit mistakes, and put the long-term health of the business ahead of short term pressures?
- **Talent**: do they have the skill, vision, and discipline to grow the business and navigate challenges?

### Assessing integrity

What to read: the annual shareholder letter (the CEO reflects on the year, highlights wins and losses, outlines strategy), earnings calls (key decisions, concerns, outlook), and official filings, especially the Business Description and Management's Discussion and Analysis.

Questions to answer:

- Do they explain complex issues clearly, or are they hiding behind jargon?
- Do they explain why revenue or profits changed, or just state that they did?
- Do they offer a measured outlook on the future, or just broad, optimistic statements?
- Are they open about challenges, or do they only focus on the positives?
- When something goes wrong, do they take responsibility, or blame external factors?
- Do they sound like a thoughtful leader, or more like a politician dodging hard questions?
- After reading, do I trust them more or less?

Good examples of the tone to look for:

- Berkshire shareholder letter: "Berkshire's per-share market value dropped by 5.3% in 2020, while the S&P 500 gained 18.4%"
- Meta annual report: "Advertising revenue in 2024 increased $29.68 billion, or 22% compared to 2023 due to increases in ad impressions delivered and average price per ad."
- Meta annual report: "Some of these investments, particularly our significant investments in Reality Labs, have generated only limited revenue and reduced our operating margin and profitability, and we expect the adverse financial impact of such investments to continue for the foreseeable future."

### Watch how CEOs are paid

To understand a CEO's priorities, follow the money. Executive compensation is detailed in SEC Form DEF 14A. Look at how much they earn in each category, and what conditions must be met for them to get paid.

- **Good**: CEO owns 20% of the company, takes no salary, and receives only long-term restricted stock units and performance stock units tied to multiyear shareholder returns.
- **Bad**: CEO with little to no equity ownership, a $20 million salary, and a $10 million cash bonus if earnings per share increase by 10% next year.

### Assessing talent

Assessing talent is really about assessing the ability to allocate capital wisely. Three areas:

1. **Return on invested capital**: use the Capital Efficiency Test.
2. **Dividends and buybacks**: great when timed wisely. If a company earns a 40% ROIC there is no reason to pay dividends or buy back stock, because a dividend forces the shareholder to go find another 40% return, which is not easy. Dividends suit retirees or people who need the money, but for long-term investment it is better for management to compound at higher rates of return. If a company cannot reinvest capital at attractive rates, a dividend is the right thing to do, which is largely irrelevant because you should not be investing in that company anyway. Buybacks should only happen at a discount to intrinsic value, and management should capitalize when ROIC is high. The worst possibility is management reinvesting in low-return projects for the sake of growth.
3. **Debt management**: are they using leverage responsibly or recklessly? Where does the capital come from?

Two financial checks:

**Leverage** - whether the company has taken on excessive debt.

```
Debt-to-equity ratio = Total Debt / Shareholders' Equity
```

Total debt is short term plus long term debt; shareholders' equity is net assets after liabilities. A high value is acceptable in stable, cash-generating businesses like utilities, but a red flag in cyclical or unpredictable industries. Below 1 is conservative, above 2 is high. Criterion: below 1.

**Liquidity** - whether the company has enough cash and short-term assets to cover its obligations.

```
Current ratio = Current Assets / Current Liabilities
```

Current assets are anything expected to convert to cash within a year; current liabilities are short-term debts, accounts payable, accrued expenses, and other obligations due within 12 months. A ratio of 1 is exactly enough to cover liabilities, 2 or more is a healthy margin of safety, and below 1 is a red flag. Companies with steady cash flow or rapid inventory turnover can sit just above 1, while capital intensive companies like manufacturers should hold more. Criterion: above 1.5.
