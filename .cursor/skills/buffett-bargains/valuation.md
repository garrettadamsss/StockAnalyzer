# Valuation

After identifying a great company, estimate its intrinsic value so you know when to invest. No matter how great the company is, it is not worth an infinite price.

The key method is discounted cash flow analysis. Imagine buying the entire company today, owning it for 10 years, collecting the cash flow it generates during that time, then selling the business to another investor after 10 years.

`scripts/dcf_valuation.py` performs all six steps. This document explains what the script is doing so its output can be sanity-checked and its assumptions defended.

## Step 1: Project future cash flows

Take the current cash flow and grow it at the expected annual growth rate for 10 years.

```
FCF(n) = FCF(0) x (1 + g)^n
```

With $1,000 of initial cash flow growing 10% a year, year 10 cash flow is $2,593 and the 10 years together produce $17,531.

## Step 2: Estimate the terminal value

Terminal value is what you expect to sell the business for after 10 years.

```
Terminal Value = exit multiple x year 10 cash flow
```

At a 20x multiple: `20 x $2,593 = $51,874`. Total cash generated by the business is the free cash flow received over 10 years plus the sale proceeds: `$17,531 + $51,874 = $69,406`.

## Step 3: Determine the price to pay today

You should pay less than that total, because money received in the future is worth less than money received today. Target a 15% annual return; anything less and a passive index fund is the better choice. Discount every future cash flow and the terminal value:

```
PV = FV / (1 + r)^n
```

- `PV` is present value
- `FV` is the cash expected in the future
- `r` is the discount rate, the desired annual return
- `n` is the number of years until the cash flow arrives

For example, `PV = $2,143.59 / (1 + 0.15)^8 = $700.74`. Investing $700.74 today at a 15% annual return for 8 years produces $2,143.59.

## Step 4: Add the discounted cash flows

```
Intrinsic Value = discounted cash flows of years 1 through 10 + discounted terminal value
```

Continuing the example: `$7,895 + $12,822 = $20,717.72`.

## Step 5: Adjust for cash and debt

Add cash and equivalents, subtract total debt.

```
Intrinsic Value = discounted cash flows + discounted terminal value + cash - debt
```

With $2,000 of cash and $5,000 of debt: `$20,717.72 + $2,000 - $5,000 = $17,717`.

## Step 6: Apply a margin of safety

Anything can happen over 10 years to reduce the projected growth rate, so take an arbitrary percentage off intrinsic value to account for that unpredictability. Aim for a minimum of 30%.

```
Final entry price = Intrinsic Value - (Intrinsic Value x Margin of Safety)
```

`$17,717 - ($17,717 x 0.30) = $12,400`.

The key is to wait patiently for those rare moments when the market offers a high-quality business at a steep discount, then swing hard. Margin of safety is what separates speculation from smart investing, and it gives you the confidence to act when opportunity knocks.

## Defaults and overrides

| Input | Default | When to override |
|-------|---------|------------------|
| Base free cash flow | Latest fiscal year from `fetch_metrics.py` | Use a normalized 3-year average when the latest year is distorted by one-off items |
| Growth rate | 10% | Lower it when the Two Engine test fails or revenue growth is decelerating; raise it only with evidence from the business narrative |
| Terminal multiple | 20x | Lower for capital intensive, cyclical, or low-growth businesses |
| Discount rate | 15% | Keep it; it is the required return, not a risk model output |
| Margin of safety | 30% | Increase it when the business is harder to forecast |

Every override must be stated in the report along with its justification. When the estimate is fragile, run `--sensitivity` to show entry prices across a grid of growth rates and terminal multiples rather than presenting a single number with false precision.

## Reconciling to a share price

The script divides intrinsic value by diluted shares outstanding when `--shares` is supplied, and compares the margin of safety entry price to `--price`. Without both, report the entry price as a whole-company figure and say explicitly that no per-share comparison was made.
